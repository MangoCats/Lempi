Appendix: How the Program Director Decides

You can't directly control most of what's described here — this is the machinery behind automatic selection, included so you can understand why Lempi played what it played, not so you can operate it directly. Where you genuinely can affect something, it says so and points back to where.

Two separate questions, kept separate

Every time Lempi needs to pick the next track, it's really answering two independent questions: how often is a given song allowed to come up (timing), and does it fit right now (character — the mood/style of the moment). The code keeps these strictly apart on purpose — a song's timing never influences whether it sounds right for the moment, and vice versa — which is also why the "why this passage" panel (Advanced Features) reports them as separate line items rather than one blended score.

How often: Cooldown, Recovery, Preference

This is the math behind the three dials in Preference Tuning, which you can set directly, per recording and per artist:

  • Cooldown is a flat exclusion: once a song plays, it is completely ineligible until its cooldown period has passed. Not "less likely" — genuinely off the table.
  • Recovery begins right when cooldown ends: the song is technically eligible again but starts near zero likelihood and climbs back to its normal odds over the recovery period, rather than snapping instantly from "never" to "certain."
  • Preference is a standing multiplier applied on top of all of that, independent of timing — how much you generally want to hear this at all.

Both Cooldown and Recovery are stored on a stretchy scale rather than a plain number of hours, which is what lets the slider in Preference Tuning stay useful across a huge range — small adjustments near the middle change things gently, and each step further out roughly doubles or halves the actual time, so extreme values are reachable without the useful middle ground being crushed into a sliver of the slider.

An artist's own Cooldown/Recovery/Preference apply on top of each of their individual recordings' own settings, multiplied together — so turning an artist down quiets everything of theirs at once, even a specific song you'd separately turned up.

A few smaller effects feed into "how often" too: a mild preference toward typically-sized songs over very short or very long ones; a "related recordings" link so a live version and a studio version of the same song share some of the same cooldown, rather than one letting the other straight back in; and skipping or removing a track from the queue holds it back for its own separate, usually shorter window (the "Hold a skipped/removed track out for…" settings in Advanced Features) — once that window passes, a skip has no further effect on the song's future odds.

Time of year

Separately from all of the above, a song can carry a seasonal character — the clearest example is holiday music. A curve maps the day of year to a multiplier, and a song's own "how seasonal is this" value decides how strongly that multiplier applies to it specifically; a song with no seasonal character at all is untouched year-round. This is why certain songs seem to show up only around a particular time of year, entirely apart from anything in Preference Tuning.

Does it fit right now: flavor

Every recording has a large set of measurements describing what it actually sounds like (see "Flavor analysis" in Importing & Refining Music) — mood, genre character, energy, and more. Comparing two songs' measurements gives a single "how different do these sound" number. This does two jobs, and never affects how often something is allowed to play — only whether it's the right fit at this moment:

  • Which songs are even in the running. A program (see below) supplies a small handful of example songs that define its sound; the pool of candidates is narrowed to whatever sounds similar to at least one of them. If you've ever marked something as a Like, it acts as an extra, gentler example pulling the pool toward your taste; a Dislike instead pushes anything too similar to it out of the running.
  • What plays right after what. Among whatever is eligible, songs that sound closer to whatever's already queued next are favored, so one track blends into the next rather than lurching.

Programs and the schedule

A program is a named block of time (like "morning" or "evening") defined by a start time and a short list of example songs that define what it should sound like — not a set of sliders, and not a genre tag. "What should this time of day sound like?" is answered by naming a handful of songs, and the flavor-matching described above pulls the rest of the selection toward that same sound. Switching from one program to the next at its scheduled start is a clean cut, not a fade between two sounds — any sense of continuity across that switch comes entirely from the next-song blending described above, not from the schedule itself.

You can pick a program by hand or return to "automatic" from any skin (Advanced Features) — but defining what a program actually contains isn't something the web interface can do today. If your system has programs already, they were set up separately from the web UI; picking among them is the only lever you currently have.
